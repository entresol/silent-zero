PACE, provenance-aware capability enforcement

PACE: tool calls checked against a capability compiled from the authenticated request; 0 of 30 out-of-authority goals succeeded in a reduced adaptive search; premises unproved. test, full text.

A preprint. arXiv. The source code is said to be in the supplemental material.

Read: the whole text except the reference list. The proofs were read and agree with the main text’s statements; they are not independently verified.

What it shows

  • The argument against admission-time review (§3, Theorem 1). A skill or tool description can be safe or leaking with identical code and interface, and only its natural-language specification differs. If the review evidence cannot tell such variants apart (condition C1) and an attacker can build a context in which the leaking one violates a noninterference-style criterion (C2), then a sound gate must keep mediating that egress site for both. The authors say neither condition is a claim about every admission system; Proposition D.1 gives a positive case where a code-resolved judgment may relax a site, which does not apply to the mail skill (D.1). The conclusion they draw is to decide at the last tool boundary, before an external effect.
  • The mechanism (§4). For every tool call PACE freezes the arguments, records provenance in an append-only graph, and runs two checks. Path confinement proposes a minimum cut of the represented influence paths from protected or untrusted sources to an unsafe effect; the verifier checks that the cut separates and is enforceable, not that it is minimal (“recomputed separation and enforceability, not recomputed optimality”), so minimality remains declared (§4.2, D.2, C.4). On AgentDyn, 5,842 nonempty cuts led to no executed nonterminal rewrite, and utility came back through restoration of terminal blocks (D.3, C.5, F.2). Capability and effect verification checks nine obligations over each call’s schema-declared effects against a capability compiled from the authenticated request (purpose, resource, arguments, budget, expiry, delegation depth). That capability is read only from the trusted request channel, so model-generated text cannot mint or widen it (§4.1). Allowed actions are allow, block, refuse, dry run, read-only view, argument rewrite, sanitisation and budget cap; rewriting is rarely licensed, so PACE blocks at most sites (§4.3, C.5).
  • Two configurations (§4). PACE-C is the certified contract. PACE-P is what every experiment evaluates; it can restore a call that path confinement blocked when the effect check accepts it, apply a declared repair, and release low-impact calls (rule R2) by a fast path, without recertifying the final call; restored calls skip the cut, so in the evaluated configuration not every call gets the full authority check (§4.2, C.6, D.6). The authors state that the decision rule specifies the certified contract and is not a claim that every evaluated dispatch satisfies it.
  • Guarantees (§4.4). Under stated premises, accepted certificates give separation of the represented paths and coverage of schema-exposed effects by valid capabilities (Proposition 1); a conditional theorem says a covered, emitted and simulated chain from a protected source to the unsafe effect cannot complete at that call (Theorem 2). The authors say plainly that neither establishes noninterference, and that observable refusal remains a channel with no bound proved.
  • Benchmark results (§5.2, Table 1). Eight executable benchmarks (AgentDojo, AgentDyn, WASP, InjecAgent, ASB, PASB, MCPTox, MSB), three target models at temperature 0 (gpt-5.6-luna, DeepSeek-V4-Flash, a local Qwen-3.8-27B). Among 79 eligible per-group attack-success columns, PACE-P is strictly lowest in 62, tied lowest in 14 and higher in three (Appendix F.1 names them). Native utility drops by at most 2.92 points on the full benchmarks; MCPTox refusal differs by at most 1.81 points. On AgentDojo, worst-group attack success for Qwen, DeepSeek and the GPT model was 0.0, 5.09 and 8.11 per cent against 48.0, 99.84 and 100.0 undefended, with utility 59.67, 75.25 and 72.05 against 62.59, 75.68 and 73.77 (Table 1). On AgentDojo the text reports lower worst-group attack success and higher utility than CaMeL, MELON and Progent on all three models.
  • Residuals (§5.2). On Qwen, 4.76 and 7.14 per cent on WASP, 0.42 on ASB direct injection, 2.50 on MSB-Call. In Table 10 the data-stealing second stage on the GPT model is 30.77 per cent for PACE against 50.50 undefended; the authors exclude that conditional metric from the 79-column count.
  • Ablation (§5.3, Table 2). 1,167 paired cases, Qwen only, eight arms switching path confinement (P), capability and effect verification (C) and boundary adaptation (B). The authors conclude that effect verification carries most of the security gain and boundary adaptation prevents indiscriminate refusal. They also say several reduced arms tie the full system’s floor, that the full combination “has no demonstrated unique security benefit”, and that on this subset AgentDyn utility falls from 66.7 to 33.3 per cent, so the three-point bound does not hold there. ASB and InjecAgent-Enhanced are zero in every arm. It is one deterministic run with one seed, described as descriptive; no component effect is claimed significant (E.4).
  • Adaptive search (§5.2, E.2, F.2). From 629 AgentDojo (user, injection) pairs, an audit found 375 (59.6 per cent) whose target call fails the authority check without any defence. Ten such pairs each from Slack, Travel and Workspace were attacked on Qwen, with a mutator (the same checkpoint at temperature 1.0) proposing four new payloads per round, up to 50 episodes per pair. Undefended, 21 of 30 succeeded (70.0 per cent, Wilson interval 52.1 to 83.3), nine of them first with search-generated payloads. Against PACE-P, 0 of 30 (interval 0 to 11.4 per cent) after 1,500 episodes; 157 target calls were all blocked before dispatch, in 126 episodes across 14 pairs: 149 failed the authority and budget checks (AUTH and BUDGET) and eight additionally failed BIND and CTRL (F.2); none went through the fast path.

How

PACE is a mediator that a host places between a frozen agent and its tools; it needs per-tool schemas with declared effects, adapters that expose hooks, and a deployment that declares which severing actions are safe. Capabilities were compiled deterministically from the benchmark’s request, excluding attack labels and judge outputs; policies and schemas were developed by the authors from public interfaces, task formats and development tests, then frozen before the final runs (E.2). Baselines (CaMeL, FIDES, DRIFT, Progent, MELON, DataSentinel, prompt-level defences and others) were run locally at pinned revisions, natively on AgentDojo and as native or validated ports elsewhere, with DTA a faithful instrumentation reference (E.5, Table 7). Attack success is judged by a frozen model judge with evidence required and no manual correction; the paper gives no judge-agreement figures (E.4).

Limits

The authors state the following.

  • The experiments evaluate PACE-P, not the certified PACE-C. Its guarantee for the evaluated configuration depends on the final action (§4.4); there is no general episode invariant for it.
  • Guarantees concern represented chains. Four cases link a real dependence to nothing in the graph: undeclared reads, secondary external calls inside a tool, strings the model recreates, and resource changes that bypass versioned adapters. The re-encoding check catches deterministic transformations only (base64, hex, digests and the like); paraphrase, translation and short fragments escape, and short low-entropy values give false matches (C.6).
  • Request compilation, effect labels, validators, source labels and severance contracts are premises that run counts do not establish. The verifier shares code with the builder, so both agree on every semantic omission (C.6). Nothing defends against a compromised mediation layer (C.6).
  • Residual attacker goals can lie inside the compiled authority, use declared repairs or the R2 class, exploit unrepresented channels or break those premises (C.6).
  • The adaptive test covers only out-of-authority goals in three suites, one target model, one mutator and 50 episodes per case; stronger mutators and attacks on parsing or on unrepresented transformations are untested (F.2). The authors call it reduced scale.
  • Corollary D.2 holds only for PACE-C and needs H2 and A1 to A9; the integrity version of Corollary D.1 is weaker in practice; D.4 and D.5 rest on protocol assumptions A1 to A9 that are not part of the evaluated artifact.
  • Durable reservation, one-shot tokens and crash recovery are specified but outside the evaluated artifact (§4.3). No numerical overhead of mediation is reported (E.7). Future work is semantic restatement, fragmented flows, hidden secondary effects and durable distributed execution (§6).

A careful reader adds the following (inference).

  • Table 1 compares the evaluated PACE-P with ported baselines, and the main tables carry no confidence intervals (E.4). The three models are one commercial name, one provider’s API model and one open model; the ablation and the adaptive search use only the open one.
  • By the audit, 254 of the 629 AgentDojo pairs (our subtraction) are not out of authority. The 0 of 30 says nothing about those, where the residual rates of Table 1 live and where the check offers no cut by itself.
  • The capabilities and schemas were developed by the authors for each benchmark. How much of the result carries to a deployment that must write them for open-ended tasks is not tested.
  • The abstract’s “most security gains to effect verification” and the section’s “no demonstrated unique security benefit” for the full combination should be read together.
  • A preprint, with no peer review stated.

Against the wiki

  • The broker: extends. This is a measured mediator of the same shape as the paper’s broker, a deterministic check of calls against authority taken from the human’s request, with budget, expiry and delegation depth as bounds; in the evaluated configuration, low-impact calls (rule R2) and declared repairs can pass without full authority checking, and restored calls skip the cut (§4.2, C.6, D.6). It speaks to the open question of who writes the policies: here the authors’ compiler intersects request-derived effect families, argument constraints, organisational policy and tool schemas (E.2), with schemas and policy developed by the authors from public interfaces, task formats and development tests, then frozen (E.2). Like the broker, it treats the mediation layer as the trusted base without proving it (C.6). The paper says only that the prototype mediates one in-process execution path (C.3) and that the defender acts at the tool boundary (§2.1); our reading: it is software at a host boundary, with no kernel below it. See also ToolFence, which is of the same family.
  • Prompt injection: extends. Adds an adaptive test of a defence around the model on a small scale, a 30-pair search on one open model against out-of-authority goals only, with 0 of 30 successes. It is consistent with the page’s statement that evaluation can only fail to break a defence (The Attacker Moves Second, §6); it is less than the human, gradient and reinforcement-learning attacks that paper used. See adaptive evaluation of out-of-band defenses: both tests attack a deterministic check with an automated search and both are small.
  • Information-flow control: extends. PACE adds a graph cut over declared flows to authority checks on effects, and states that it does not give noninterference. Its provenance labels come from deployment-assigned origins and are a stated premise (C.6), which matches the survey’s point that the first label is the trusted base.
  • Model Context Protocol: extends. MCP tool descriptions are named as an attacker-authored channel (§1). On MCPTox, worst-group attack success for PACE was 0.0, 2.62 and 2.65 per cent against 32.52, 56.54 and 86.93 undefended, with refusal 1.97, 0.58 and 28.55 against 0.16, 0.42 and 27.96 (Table 1). That page says its sources measure how often attacks work and not what a mediator changes; this one measures a mediator, on a benchmark with its own attack construction.
  • Object capabilities: related, not the same. The authors’ capabilities are records compiled from the request and checked by the host; Our reading: the agent does not hold or pass them; the paper does not describe how delegation works (delegation depth appears in §4.1 and §4.2 unexplained). Our reading, too: they are closer to a per-request policy than to unforgeable tokens that designate a resource.
  • Confused deputy: extends. The authors name attacks through tool metadata as a confused deputy and describe a “trust inversion” where the reviewed string is also the authority the agent obeys (§1). Our reading: their remedy takes authority from the request channel and not from what the agent is given, which is a different route from Hardy’s.
  • Defeating Prompt Injections by Design (CaMeL): related. CaMeL is a baseline; in these runs PACE has lower worst-group attack success and higher utility on AgentDojo for all three models (§5.2). The two use different models and tool setups from CaMeL’s own paper, so this is not a replication of its figures.

For Silent Zero

The broker: no change of state. It is a measured example of the check the broker is meant to perform at tool calls, and of its limits (the evaluated configuration lets low-impact calls and declared repairs pass without the full check): the check is only as good as the effect schemas, the request compiler and complete mediation, which the paper states as premises (compare the paper’s own requirement that the tool adapters leave no path around the broker, Section 6). Our reading: it supports the paper’s choice to keep the broker in the trusted base and to label it tested, not proven, and it shows why the guarantee covers only declared effects. It does not alter the statement that Silent Zero does not solve prompt injection: goals inside the granted authority pass by design (§5.2, C.6). The source’s certified contract is a proof-carrying scheme whose theorems we did not read, and its runtime sits above a conventional host; nothing in Silent Zero is built.
